# Reference Unit 4: Chapter III — Attack by Stratagem

## Core Idea

Chapter III ranks preservation and strategic disruption above ruinous direct attack, warns against siege, and ties sound action to knowledge of self and opponent.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Preservation Ranking**: Prefer achieving the objective while preserving valuable systems and relationships. (III.1-4)
  - When to use: Destruction is being confused with success.
  - How: Identify what must remain intact after victory.
- **Avoid-the-Siege Test**: Search for changes to plan, alliance, incentives, or position before frontal escalation. (III.5-10)
  - When to use: Direct contest is expensive and slow.
  - How: Map non-destructive paths and their ethical constraints.
- **Two-Sided Knowledge Audit**: Separate what is known about one's own capacity and the counterpart. (III.17-18)

## Key Concepts

- **Strategic attack**: Action against plans, alliances, or capacity rather than direct destruction.
- **Siege**: Costly frontal method used as a warning case.
- **Self/opponent knowledge**: Comparative understanding, not omniscience.

## Anti-patterns

- **Cleverness fetish**: Preferring indirectness even when direct transparency is best.
- **Preservation rhetoric**: Claiming restraint while imposing hidden harm.

## Worked Example

A team facing internal resistance changes the rollout sequence, recruits affected operators into design, and pilots reversibly instead of forcing an all-at-once mandate.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define what must survive success.
2. Examine indirect paths before escalation.
3. Audit both internal and external assumptions.
4. Do not let indirection obscure accountability.
