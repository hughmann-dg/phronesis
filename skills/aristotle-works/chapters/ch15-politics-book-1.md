# Politics — Book I

## Core Idea

Political association is examined by decomposing the city into households, roles, property, and forms of rule, with the city presented as ordered toward living well.

## Reference Map

- City and association ordered toward a good; method of analyzing constituent parts (chs. 1–2).
- Household relations and Aristotle's arguments about slavery (chs. 2–7).
- Property acquisition, use, exchange, money, and wealth-getting (chs. 8–11).
- Household authority and moral education (chs. 12–13).

## Decision Rules

- Analyze a political whole through its constituent relationships without assuming that rule in household and city is identical (chs. 1–2).
- Distinguish using a thing for its characteristic function from exchanging it for gain (chs. 8–9).
- Distinguish provision for household needs from unlimited accumulation pursued as an end (chs. 9–11).

## Critical Limits

- Aristotle's defense of “natural slavery,” hierarchy of sexes, and exclusion from citizenship are morally unacceptable and empirically unsupported. Study them as historical arguments, never as executable guidance (chs. 3–7, 12–13).
- Claims about what is “natural” require independent ethical and empirical review.
