# The Categories

## Core Idea

The work distinguishes ways terms are said, ways things relate to subjects, and basic kinds of predication so that reasoning does not slide between different senses or ontological roles.

## Reference Map

- **Equivocal, univocal, derivative naming**: Section 1, Part 1.
- **Said of / present in a subject**: Section 1, Parts 2–3.
- **Ten categories**: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, affection (Section 1, Part 4).
- **Substance**: primary and secondary substance and their distinctive status (Section 1, Part 5).
- **Quantity**: discrete/continuous and relative/non-relative quantity (Section 1, Part 6).
- **Relation and quality**: kinds and diagnostic difficulties (Section 2, Parts 7–8).
- **Post-predicaments**: opposition, priority, simultaneity, motion, and having (Section 3, Parts 10–15).

## Decision Rules

- Before debating a term, test whether participants use one name with different definitions (Part 1).
- Distinguish what is predicated of a subject from what inheres in a subject (Parts 2–3).
- Do not infer that every grammatical predicate names a substance; first identify its category (Part 4).
- When comparing claims, check whether the alleged opposites are contradictories, contraries, relatives, or possession/privation (Part 10).

## Anti-patterns

- Treating equivocal uses as though they shared one definition.
- Reifying a quality, relation, or quantity as an independently existing primary substance.
- Using “prior” without specifying whether priority is temporal, explanatory, existential, or otherwise ordered.
