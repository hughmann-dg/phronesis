# Patterns

These are reconstructed inquiry patterns, not claims that Aristotle used modern framework names.

## End–Means Hierarchy

**When to use**: A decision has many metrics or intermediate goals.

**How**:

1. List the ends each action pursues.
2. Mark which ends are chosen for something further.
3. Identify the most final end relevant to the decision.
4. Reject means that defeat or corrupt that end.

**Trade-offs**: Clarifies purpose but does not by itself settle disagreements about the good. (Ethics I, chs. 1–2)

## Context-Sensitive Mean

**When to use**: A response can fail by excess, deficiency, wrong timing, wrong object, or wrong manner.

**How**:

1. Name the field of feeling or action.
2. Identify characteristic excesses and deficiencies.
3. Account for the person's role, resources, relationships, and circumstances.
4. Use experienced judgment to choose the fitting response.
5. Correct for the extreme toward which existing habit is biased.

**Trade-offs**: Not an arithmetic average and not applicable to acts wrong in themselves. (Ethics II, chs. 6–9)

## Particulars-First Deliberation

**When to use**: General rules underdetermine contingent action.

**How**:

1. State the worthwhile end.
2. Separate fixed facts from variables action can affect.
3. Gather the particulars that materially change fit.
4. Generate achievable means.
5. Test means for both effectiveness and consistency with good character.
6. Choose, act, and revise as particulars change.

**Trade-offs**: Judgment can be biased; pair with evidence and independent review. (Ethics III, ch. 5; VI, chs. 5, 7–13)

## Appearance–Puzzle–Resolution

**When to use**: A concept is surrounded by conflicting reputable opinions.

**How**:

1. State the observed or widely accepted appearances.
2. Surface contradictions and hard cases.
3. Distinguish senses of ambiguous terms.
4. Resolve the puzzles while preserving the strongest grounded appearances.
5. Label what remains unsettled.

**Trade-offs**: Reputable opinion is evidence, not authority. (Ethics VII, ch. 1)

## Justice-Type Diagnostic

**When to use**: Parties agree an outcome is unfair but disagree on remedy.

**How**:

1. Decide whether the issue is distribution, correction, exchange, or general lawfulness.
2. State the proposed basis of equality or desert.
3. Identify who gains, loses, and controls the comparison.
4. Apply proportional allocation or corrective restoration as appropriate.
5. Test whether equity must correct an over-general rule.

**Trade-offs**: The legitimate basis of desert requires independent moral argument. (Ethics V)

## Constitution Decomposition

**When to use**: A regime label hides how power actually operates.

**How**:

1. Map who participates in deliberation, offices, and courts.
2. Record selection, eligibility, tenure, pay, audit, and removal.
3. Identify the interest the arrangement actually serves.
4. Analyze the social groups and material conditions beneath it.
5. Test stability, accountability, and feasible transition paths.

**Trade-offs**: Aristotle's restricted citizenship must not be imported into modern use. (Politics III–VI; Athenian Constitution, Parts 42–69)

## Feasible-Best Ladder

**When to use**: An ideal design cannot be implemented immediately.

**How**:

1. Describe the best arrangement in principle.
2. Describe the best fit for the actual people and conditions.
3. Describe the best attainable change from the current state.
4. Specify preservation conditions and failure modes.

**Trade-offs**: Feasibility can become an excuse for avoidable injustice; retain moral constraints. (Politics IV, ch. 1)

## Causal Plot Test

**When to use**: Designing or evaluating a narrative, scenario, or explanation.

**How**:

1. Identify the central action.
2. Remove incidents that do not affect the whole.
3. Test whether each transition follows by probability or necessity.
4. Derive reversal and recognition from prior incidents.
5. Test whether the ending resolves the action rather than bypassing it.

**Trade-offs**: A narrative model is not empirical proof. (Poetics, chs. VII–XVII)

## Comparative Observation Matrix

**When to use**: Organizing varied empirical cases before causal explanation.

**How**:

1. Define comparison axes such as part, function, habitat, motion, season, or development.
2. Record similarities, differences, degrees, and exceptions.
3. Separate direct observation from report and inference.
4. Use exceptions to refine group boundaries.
5. Recheck all ancient biological conclusions against modern science.

**Trade-offs**: Classification can encode poor observations and obsolete categories. (History of Animals I–IX)
