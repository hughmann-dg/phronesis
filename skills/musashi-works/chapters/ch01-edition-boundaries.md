# Reference Unit 1: Edition Boundaries

## Core Idea

The verified source is a Japanese text keyed to a 1909 parent edition. Accurate use requires language, transcription, and interpretive boundaries to remain visible.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Three-Layer Check**: Separate Japanese primary text, English interpretive synthesis, and executable behavior.
  - When to use: Every source-grounded answer.
  - How: Retrieve the Japanese paragraph, cite its scroll and subsection, then label any English rendering as a gloss or synthesis.
- **Scan Verification**: Check exact wording against the NDL scan before quoting.
  - When to use: Direct quotation, philological claims, or disputed readings.
  - Why: The access transcription reports correcting obvious errors and consulting another edition.

## Key Concepts

- **Parent edition**: The 1909 Kinkodo volume identified by the transcription and NDL record.
- **Access transcription**: The Koten.net original-text pages assembled into the local ignored corpus.
- **Named subsection**: The stable locator beneath one of the five scrolls.
- **English synthesis**: Project-authored interpretation, not a translation licensed from a modern edition.

## Anti-patterns

- **Translation laundering**: Presenting a modern translator's English as though it came from the public-domain Japanese source.
- **Edition collapse**: Treating all manuscript families and printed editions as identical.
- **Unmarked gloss**: Giving an English phrase quotation marks without a verified English source.

## Worked Example

A decision memo explains rhythm. It cites Earth scroll, "Rhythm in Strategy," calls its English wording a synthesis, and links an exact quotation only after checking the 1909 scan.

## Key Takeaways

1. The source is Japanese only.
2. Cite scroll and subsection.
3. Verify quotations against the scan.
4. Keep interpretation and behavior separate.
