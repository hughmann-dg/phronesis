# Rolleston Book II - Judgment, Training, and Commitment

## Core Idea

Sound agency requires coordinated training of pursuit and avoidance, fitting action within relationships, and assent to impressions; a commitment is credible only after its price and consequences have been considered.

## Reference Map

- Beliefs held as conclusions versus borrowed school positions (ch. 1).
- Circumstances as material for one's use; appearances require judgment (chs. 2-3, 7).
- Three fields of exercise: pursuit/avoidance, fitting conduct, and assent (ch. 4).
- Confidence about agency combined with caution in using circumstances (chs. 5-6).
- Identity, self-knowledge, and the external sources of dependency (chs. 8-10, 16-19).
- Endurance, self-sufficiency, roles, and relative valuation (chs. 20-26).
- Prerequisites and costs of a serious commitment (ch. 27).

## Decision Rules

- Separate a belief supported by one's examined reasons from a slogan borrowed from a group (ch. 1).
- Keep caution where error is possible—in assent and conduct—while refusing panic about what is not wholly one's act (chs. 5-7).
- Test an appearance before it enters a chain of imagined consequences (ch. 7).
- Identify which external reward, fear, or approval has become a bond on judgment (chs. 16-19).
- Compare gains by their actual category: money, status, freedom from flattery, integrity, time, or skill should not be collapsed into one scale without argument (chs. 21 and 24).
- Before accepting a role or project, enumerate preparation, recurring discipline, setbacks, opportunity costs, and fit with ability (ch. 27).
- Prefer a coherent commitment to serial identification with whatever is currently admired (ch. 27).

## Three-Field Diagnostic

1. **Pursuit and avoidance**: Is success defined by obtaining or escaping something another controls?
2. **Fitting conduct**: What action is responsible in this relationship and role?
3. **Assent**: Which impressions have been accepted without sufficient testing?

Rolleston's ch. 4 also warns that advanced argument about assent cannot compensate for untrained desire or conduct.

## Worked Example

A person is considering a prestigious job that requires relocation. The first field identifies outcome demands: being admired, securing rapid advancement, or ensuring the relationship survives unchanged. The second asks what conduct is owed to a partner, future team, health, and existing commitments. The third tests impressions such as "this is my only chance" or "declining proves cowardice." Chapter 27 then prices the role: relocation, workload, travel, recovery time, and likely reversals. A three-month trial may emerge not as indecision but as a designed way to test costs while preserving truthful commitments.

## Limits

The chapter's athletic and Cynic examples can encourage heroic overreach. Modern use must account for disability, caregiving, economic constraint, power imbalance, and duties to others. Integrity is not measured by maximum deprivation.
