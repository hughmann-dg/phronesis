# Encheiridion 15-33 - Participation, Roles, and Commitment Costs

## Core Idea

Participate in social life without making status or another person's conduct the condition of one's own judgment; derive action from roles and duties, and count the full price of commitments before adopting them.

## Reference Map

- Banquet, sympathy, and actor metaphors for participation in circumstances (15-18).
- Avoiding contests whose decisive condition belongs to others; freedom from status comparison (19-23).
- Civic usefulness, office, integrity, and the price of social rewards (24-25).
- Consistency between judgments about one's own and another's losses (26-28).
- Prerequisites and consequences of an undertaking (29).
- Duties discovered from relationships; self-interest and social interest joined (30).
- Ancient religious practice and divination, bounded by reasoned duty (31-32).
- A prescribed public and private form of conduct (33).

## Decision Rules

- Participate without grabbing, clinging, or treating delay as humiliation (15).
- Offer humane sympathy while guarding against untested conclusions; do not use the text to police another person's grief (16).
- Focus on acting a role well, but challenge any assignment that violates rights, safety, or justice (17).
- Do not enter a status contest whose victory depends entirely on another's ranking (19).
- Define civic usefulness through faithful performance and contribution, not office or prestige alone (24).
- Ask what price—flattery, time, compromise, exposure—purchased a reward before envying it (25).
- Apply the same general description to comparable events involving oneself and others, then account for morally relevant differences (26).
- Before committing, examine prerequisites, consequences, costs, and fit with ability (29).
- Derive a candidate duty from the relationship, then test it against independent ethical and safety constraints (30).
- When reason already identifies a duty to friend or community, do not outsource it to prediction (32).

## Worked Example

A founder envies a competitor's access to influential investors. Section 25 asks for the actual price: networking time, messaging concessions, governance terms, and attention diverted from customers. The founder can choose to pay that price, negotiate a different route, or retain the unspent time and independence. Section 24 then asks how the founder can still serve employees and customers without prestige. The analysis does not declare funding unimportant; it makes the exchange explicit.

## Critical Limits

The actor metaphor can become fatalism if a socially imposed role is treated as unquestionable. Modern application preserves moral agency to refuse abuse, discrimination, domination, and unsafe work.

Section 30's examples assume ancient family and status relations. Use the relational method, not the hierarchy. Section 33 includes culture-bound prescriptions about gender, sexuality, entertainment, speech, and social display; it is historical evidence, not a universal modern conduct code.
