# Reference Unit 8: Book VII — The Attack

## Core Idea

Attack pursues a positive object but tends to lose strength through distance, garrison, resistance, supply burden, and the need to preserve gains; advance therefore has a culminating point.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Decreasing-Force Curve**: Track how each increment of advance changes support, coordination, fatigue, resistance, and retained capacity. (VII.3-5)
  - When to use: Scaling, expansion, or migration gains momentum.
  - How: Plot marginal benefit and burden; compare with consolidation and withdrawal.
- **Culminating-Point Gate**: Define when further success begins to reduce net advantage. (VII.5, VII.22)
  - When to use: Leaders interpret all continued motion as progress.
  - How: Set thresholds for support distance, quality, reserves, stakeholder resistance, and opportunity cost.
- **Positive-Object Test**: Attack requires a specific object beyond movement itself. (VII.1-3)
  - When to use: A program expands because it can.
  - How: State the desired change, necessary scope, and termination condition.

## Key Concepts

- **Strategic attack**: Action pursuing a positive objective. (VII.2-3)
- **Decreasing force**: Erosion of advantage during advance. (VII.4)
- **Culminating point of attack**: Point beyond which the balance turns. (VII.5)
- **Culminating point of victory**: Limit beyond which exploitation threatens the prior gain. (VII.22)
- **Diversion**: Secondary action whose value depends on its effect on the whole. (VII.20)

## Mental Models

- Use success as a reason to reassess, not merely accelerate.
- Think of consolidation capacity as part of expansion capacity.

## Anti-patterns

- **Momentum worship**: Treating speed as proof of remaining advantage.
- **Scope without object**: Expansion detached from the governing purpose.
- **Consolidation neglect**: Winning access without ability to support or retain it.

## Worked Example

A product expands from one segment to five. Early growth is strong, but support queues, customization, compliance, and churn rise faster than revenue. A predeclared culmination gate pauses expansion when service quality and reserve capacity cross thresholds, protecting earlier gains. (VII.4-5, VII.22)

## Key Takeaways

1. Track decreasing force during advance.
2. Define culmination before launch.
3. Reassess after success.
4. Include consolidation in the cost of growth.
