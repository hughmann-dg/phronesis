# Reference Unit 3: Section II — Origin of Ideas

## Core Idea

Hume distinguishes impressions from ideas and proposes testing ideas by tracing them to experiential sources, while acknowledging a limited counterexample involving a missing shade of color.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Copy-Principle Audit**: Ask from what impression a disputed idea derives. (II)
  - When to use: A concept is used confidently but remains undefined.
  - How: Request concrete experience, separate simple from complex ideas, and narrow or reject content that cannot be traced.
- **Simple-Complex Decomposition**: Break complex ideas into components that can be examined. (II)
  - When to use: A broad abstraction bundles several claims.
  - How: Decompose attributes, relations, examples, and counterexamples.

## Key Concepts

- **Impression**: More forceful immediate perception.
- **Idea**: Fainter perception in thought or memory.
- **Simple perception**: Element not readily decomposed further.
- **Complex idea**: Combination or transformation of simpler materials.
- **Missing shade**: Hume's acknowledged exceptional case challenging an unqualified rule.

## Mental Models

- Use examples and sensory or emotional origins to clarify abstract vocabulary.
- Preserve counterexamples supplied by the source instead of overstating the framework.

## Anti-patterns

- **Word-content confusion**: Assuming a familiar word has a clear idea behind it.
- **Exception erasure**: Presenting the copy principle as exceptionless when Section II does not.

## Worked Example

Hume asks whether someone familiar with a graded range of colors could imagine one missing shade. The case is treated as a narrow exception rather than the center of the account. In a concept audit, a single exception should qualify the rule without making the broader diagnostic useless. (II)

## Key Takeaways

1. Trace disputed ideas to experiential content.
2. Decompose complex abstractions.
3. Preserve explicit exceptions.
4. Do not mistake vocabulary for understanding.
