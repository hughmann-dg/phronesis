## Core Frameworks and Mental Models

### State the ultimate standard and the intermediate rules

Mill defends happiness as the ultimate end while allowing moral rules and cultivated character to guide ordinary action. Use outcome comparison without assuming every act should be improvised from a fresh calculation. (*Utilitarianism*, II)

### Compare quality as well as quantity

The competent-judges argument distinguishes pleasures using the informed preference of people acquainted with both. In modern analysis, specify dimensions of welfare and whose experience informs them rather than collapsing all benefits into one unit. (II)

### Count each person's good impartially

Mill's standard asks the decision-maker to consider the happiness of all concerned, not merely the agent. Map affected stakeholders and prevent organizational power from deciding whose welfare is visible. (II)

### Separate the standard of rightness from motive

Mill distinguishes what makes an act right from what motivates the actor and from how the actor's character is assessed. Evaluate consequences, intent, and trustworthiness separately. (II)

### Model internal and external sanctions

Chapter III examines social, religious, and inward motives supporting morality. In organizational decisions, distinguish enforcement, reputation, belonging, conscience, and identity rather than assuming compliance follows from argument. (III)

### Treat the proof as an argument about ends

Chapter IV argues from what people desire to happiness as an end, then explains how virtue can become part of happiness. The argument remains contested; do not present it as a deductive proof that settles all value conflict. (IV)

### Analyze justice as protected social utility

Chapter V connects justice with rules, rights, punishment, impartiality, and the unusually strong security interest people attach to them. Do not average a severe rights violation away merely because an aggregate score is positive. (V)

## Sources and Limits

Primary edition: John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism*, seventh edition, Longmans, Green, and Co., London, 1879, Project Gutenberg #11224 (`mill-utilitarianism`). Rights verification is scoped to the United States.

This skill distinguishes Mill's text from later utilitarian theories and project-authored decision procedures. Empirical outcome claims require current evidence. Aggregate benefit does not itself resolve legality, consent, dignity, basic rights, catastrophic risk, or the fair distribution of harm.
