# Reference Unit 1: Edition Boundaries

## Core Idea

The source is the 1879 seventh edition of *Utilitarianism*, reprinted from *Fraser's Magazine*. Use its five chapter headings as locators and separate Mill's argument from later utilitarian theories and project-authored decision tools.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Edition Check**: Cite the seventh edition and retrieve `mill-utilitarianism` before quoting.
  - When to use: Wording or chapter structure matters.
  - How: Confirm the claim inside the chapter body rather than Gutenberg metadata or later commentary.
- **Theory-Version Check**: Identify whether a claim belongs to Mill, later act/rule utilitarian debate, welfare economics, or this project's interpretation.
  - When to use: “Utilitarianism says” introduces a broad claim.
  - How: Name author, text, chapter, and interpretive bridge.

## Key Concepts

- **Seventh edition**: Longmans, Green, and Co., London, 1879.
- **Chapter locator**: Most stable locator in the plaintext edition.
- **Interpretation**: Project-authored reconstruction linked to but distinct from the text.
- **Modern evidence**: Required for empirical consequences unavailable to Mill.

## Anti-patterns

- **Theory collapse**: Treating all consequentialism as Mill's exact view.
- **Quotation from memory**: Using familiar slogans without edition verification.
- **Historical empirical authority**: Treating nineteenth-century observations as current data.

## Worked Example

A memo attributes a numerical cost-benefit formula to Mill. The reviewer finds no such formula in the five chapters, relabels the matrix as a modern implementation, and cites Chapter II only for the impartial consequence standard it interprets.

## Key Takeaways

1. Retrieve before quoting.
2. Cite chapter and nearby context.
3. Name later extensions.
4. Require current outcome evidence.
