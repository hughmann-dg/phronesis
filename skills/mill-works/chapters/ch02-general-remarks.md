# Reference Unit 2: Chapter I — General Remarks

## Core Idea

Chapter I frames moral philosophy's dispute over first principles, explains why ultimate ends admit a different kind of proof from ordinary facts, and announces the inquiry into utility.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ultimate-End Proof Boundary**: Do not demand the same demonstration for an ultimate end as for a factual proposition. (I)
  - When to use: A value dispute is disguised as a missing empirical fact.
  - How: Separate empirical means claims from normative end claims and identify what considerations can support each.
- **First-Principle Exposure**: Make the criterion behind moral judgments explicit. (I)
  - When to use: Rules are applied without explaining their basis or conflict resolution.
  - How: State the proposed ultimate standard and test whether intermediate rules cohere with it.

## Key Concepts

- **First principles**: Foundational standards of moral evaluation.
- **Ultimate end**: End valued for itself rather than solely as a means.
- **Proof**: Reasoned considerations appropriate to an end, not necessarily deduction.
- **Moral faculty**: Position Mill discusses and challenges as a source of general principles.

## Anti-patterns

- **Fact-value blur**: Treating measured preference as complete moral proof.
- **Axiom concealment**: Hiding value choices inside technical scoring.

## Worked Example

A hospital must choose between maximizing total treated patients and guaranteeing a minimum standard for the worst-off. More data can estimate outcomes, but the weight given to the minimum standard is a normative commitment that must be argued openly.

## Key Takeaways

1. Separate ends from empirical means.
2. Expose the criterion behind rules.
3. Do not promise deductive proof of ultimate value.
4. Keep factual uncertainty visible.
