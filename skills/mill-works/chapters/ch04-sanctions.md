# Reference Unit 4: Chapter III — Ultimate Sanction

## Core Idea

Chapter III asks what motivates adherence to utility and distinguishes external pressures from the internal feeling of duty, connecting moral motivation with social feeling.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Sanction Map**: Separate formal, social, religious, reputational, and inward sources of compliance. (III)
  - When to use: A policy is assumed to implement itself.
  - How: Identify each motivator, who controls it, and its failure or abuse mode.
- **Internalization Test**: Ask whether people understand and identify with the standard or merely comply under observation. (III)
  - When to use: Behavior changes only under enforcement.
  - How: Compare monitored and unmonitored conduct, explanation, and feedback.
- **Social-Feeling Check**: Examine whether the policy supports or erodes identification with others' interests. (III)

## Key Concepts

- **External sanction**: Motive arising from others, institutions, or religious expectation.
- **Internal sanction**: Inward feeling associated with conscience.
- **Duty**: Felt moral obligation.
- **Social feeling**: Identification with human community in Mill's account.

## Anti-patterns

- **Compliance-equals-commitment**: Mistaking monitored behavior for internal support.
- **Conscience engineering**: Manipulating identity to suppress legitimate dissent.

## Worked Example

A security policy has penalties but repeated workarounds. The sanction map finds unrealistic deadlines and weak shared ownership; the redesign fixes workflow, adds peer norms, and retains proportionate enforcement.

## Key Takeaways

1. Model implementation motives.
2. Distinguish compliance from internalization.
3. Check for coercion and perverse incentives.
4. Align systems before blaming character.
