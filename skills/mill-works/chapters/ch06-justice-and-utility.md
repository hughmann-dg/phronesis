# Reference Unit 6: Chapter V — Justice and Utility

## Core Idea

Chapter V analyzes the ideas and sentiments grouped under justice—law, rights, desert, promise, impartiality, equality, and punishment—and connects their special force with the vital social interest in security.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Justice-Element Map**: Separate violated rule, injured person, valid claim, sanction, impartial procedure, and social interest. (V)
  - When to use: “Unfair” names several different objections.
  - How: Identify which element is present and what remedy follows.
- **Right-as-Protected-Claim Test**: A right involves a claim society ought to protect. (V)
  - When to use: Aggregate benefit conflicts with a concentrated harm.
  - How: Name the claim, protection, security interest, and independent legal or moral basis.
- **Security Weighting**: Count the unusually important and durable interest in reliable protection. (V)
  - When to use: A one-off exception seems beneficial.
  - How: Add precedent, trust, vulnerability, and rule stability to consequences.

## Key Concepts

- **Justice**: Strongly binding moral domain connected with assignable claims and sanctions.
- **Right**: Claim on protection in Mill's formulation.
- **Impartiality**: Requirement arising where relevant claims should not be biased.
- **Security**: Foundational social interest supporting justice's force.

## Anti-patterns

- **Aggregate erasure**: Averaging away serious involuntary harm.
- **Rule fetish**: Ignoring cases where a rule itself is unjust.
- **Equality flattening**: Treating unequal needs as identical.

## Worked Example

A profitable data use exposes a small group to discrimination. The analysis identifies privacy and equal-treatment claims, lack of consent, trust and precedent effects, and alternatives; it does not bury the group inside total revenue.

## Key Takeaways

1. Decompose justice claims.
2. Protect assignable claims before aggregation.
3. Include security, trust, and precedent.
4. Review the justice of rules themselves.
