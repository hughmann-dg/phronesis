# Reference Unit 5: Chapter IV — What Sort of Proof the Principle of Utility Is Susceptible Of

## Core Idea

Chapter IV argues that happiness is desirable through what people in fact desire, then explains how money, power, fame, and especially virtue can become parts of happiness through association and habit.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **End-versus-Means Test**: Ask whether something is desired only instrumentally or has become part of the valued end. (IV)
  - When to use: A proxy such as revenue or status dominates the objective.
  - How: Trace what it enables, what people would sacrifice for it, and whether it crowds out the underlying good.
- **Preference-Evidence Boundary**: Actual desire is evidence about value but does not automatically settle moral justification. (IV, interpreted)
  - When to use: Popular preference is offered as sufficient proof.
  - How: Test information, adaptation, coercion, externalities, and excluded stakeholders.
- **Habit Formation Map**: Repeated means can become independently desired. (IV)

## Key Concepts

- **Desirable**: Ambiguous between capable of being desired and worthy of desire; central interpretive issue.
- **Part of happiness**: End incorporated rather than merely instrumental.
- **Virtue**: Something Mill argues can become desired for itself.
- **Will and habit**: Stabilization of conduct beyond immediate desire.

## Anti-patterns

- **Preference moralism**: Treating revealed preference as complete ethics.
- **Proxy capture**: Letting an instrumental metric replace the objective.

## Worked Example

A company treats growth as the ultimate end. The audit traces growth to stability, product reach, and employee opportunity, then identifies cases where growth degrades those goods and resets the objective hierarchy.

## Key Takeaways

1. Trace means to ends.
2. Audit when proxies become ends.
3. Treat preference as evidence, not final proof.
4. Include habit and institutional formation.
