# Reference Unit 3: Chapter II — What Utilitarianism Is

## Core Idea

Chapter II presents happiness as the standard of right action, distinguishes qualities of enjoyment, answers common objections, and separates rightness from motive while requiring regard for all affected.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Quality-and-Quantity Test**: Welfare differs in kind as well as amount. (II)
  - When to use: A single metric erases meaningful dimensions.
  - How: Identify dimensions and seek informed comparative judgment from people acquainted with both.
- **Impartial Stakeholder Scope**: Count the good of all concerned rather than the agent alone. (II)
  - When to use: Powerful stakeholders dominate visibility.
  - How: Enumerate affected groups before aggregate comparison.
- **Act-Motive-Character Split**: What makes an act right, why it was done, and what it reveals about the actor are distinct questions. (II)
  - When to use: Good intent is treated as proof of good effect.

## Key Concepts

- **Happiness**: Pleasure and absence of pain in Mill's formulation, developed qualitatively.
- **Competent judges**: Those with relevant comparative experience.
- **Secondary principles**: Moral rules shaped by accumulated experience.
- **Impartiality**: Equal consideration, not automatic equal outcome.

## Anti-patterns

- **Crude hedonism**: Reducing welfare to immediate sensation.
- **Majority utility**: Ignoring individuals because more people favor an option.
- **Motive substitution**: Evaluating consequences solely from intent.

## Worked Example

A productivity tool saves managers time but increases worker surveillance and stress. The analysis shows each stakeholder group, treats autonomy and psychological safety as qualitative welfare dimensions, and evaluates motive separately from effects.

## Key Takeaways

1. Map all affected parties.
2. Preserve qualitative welfare.
3. Use informed experience without elite fiat.
4. Separate rightness, motive, and character.
